Every program you have shipped, someone wrote. When it misbehaves you read the source, set a breakpoint, and find the line. A large language model is also a program, a few billion multiplications arranged in layers, but nobody wrote its logic. Training found it. There is no source to read, only weights: long lists of numbers.

**Mechanistic interpretability** ("mechinterp") is the attempt to read those numbers anyway. The goal is to describe, in terms a person can check, *which internal parts of a model compute what*, and *how they combine to produce a behaviour*.

<Callout tone="developer" title="The closest analogy you already know">
  Reverse engineering a compiled binary. You have the machine code, not the source. You
  disassemble it, give names to the pieces, guess what each function does, and then test your
  guess by patching the binary and watching what changes. Mechanistic interpretability does the
  same thing to a neural network.
</Callout>

## Why testing the outputs is not enough

The usual way to check a model is behavioural: give it inputs, grade the outputs. That is the black-box testing of machine learning, and it is necessary. It cannot tell you *why* the model answered as it did, so it cannot tell you whether the reason will hold on the inputs you did not test.

Two models can give the same answers on a test set for different reasons. One has learned the rule; the other has learned a shortcut that happens to agree with the rule on that data. From outside they look identical. Mechanistic interpretability looks inside to tell them apart.

## A transformer as a shared bus

The models this series is about are transformers. For our purposes, one picture is enough:

<ResidualStreamDiagram />

Each token is turned into a vector of numbers. That vector, called the **residual stream**, flows through the model. Every layer *reads* from it, computes something, and *adds* its result back. At the end, the stream is turned into a prediction for the next token.

The framework paper that made this picture popular describes the stream as a communication channel: layers talk to each other only by writing into it and reading from it ([Elhage et al., 2021](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2021/framework/index.html)). For a developer, it is a shared bus, or a blackboard every function can read and append to. Interpretability asks: what is written on the blackboard, and by whom?

## The vocabulary

Five words carry most of the field. The rest of the series builds each one with a model you can run.

| Term | What it means | Software analogy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Feature** | A property of the input the model represents, such as "this text is in French", stored as a *direction* in the residual stream | A variable, but encoded as a direction in a vector rather than a named slot |
| **Superposition** | Storing more features than there are dimensions, by letting them overlap | Bit-packing many flags into a few bytes, accepting some collisions |
| **Polysemantic neuron** | A single neuron that responds to several unrelated things, often because of superposition | A memory address reused by several variables at different times |
| **Circuit** | A set of components and connections that together carry out one behaviour | A call graph for one feature of the program |
| **Causal intervention** | Changing an internal value (setting it to zero, or swapping in a value from another input) and watching the output | Patching a running binary, or mocking one function in a test |

The last row is what makes this "mechanistic". A story about what a component does is only a hypothesis until you change that component and the behaviour changes the way the story predicts.

## What the field has found

Some results that this series will come back to:

- **Features and circuits in vision models.** The Circuits thread argued, as three explicitly speculative claims, that networks contain meaningful features, that these connect into circuits, and that similar features and circuits recur across models. It studied a vision network by "zooming in" on individual connections, an approach it compares to neuroscience and cell biology ([Olah et al., 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/zoom-in/)).
- **Superposition.** Small models trained on sparse data store more features than they have dimensions, and the way they pack them has a precise geometry ([Elhage et al., 2022](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html)). That is [part 2](/posts/features-and-superposition).
- **Pulling features out.** A sparse autoencoder decomposed one 512-neuron layer of a small language model into more than 4,000 features, such as DNA sequences, legal language and Hebrew text ([Bricken et al., 2023](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features/index.html)). The same method later reached up to 34 million features in a production model, Claude 3 Sonnet ([Templeton et al., 2024](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/index.html)). That is [part 3](/posts/sparse-autoencoders).
- **Circuits in language models.** *Induction heads*, a two-head circuit that copies patterns seen earlier in the text, appear in small transformers and may explain much of in-context learning ([Olsson et al., 2022](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/index.html)). A circuit of 26 attention heads in GPT-2 small was mapped for one specific grammatical task ([Wang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00593)). That is [part 4](/posts/induction-heads).
- **Steering.** Amplifying a single "Golden Gate Bridge" feature inside Claude 3 Sonnet made the model bring the bridge into almost every answer ([Anthropic, 2024](https://www.anthropic.com/news/golden-gate-claude)). Finding a feature, then changing it on purpose, is the strongest form of evidence the field has.

## Why a developer should care

- **It is debugging, at a new scale.** The instincts are the ones you use every day: form a hypothesis, isolate a component, change one thing, observe. The tools are new; the method is not.
- **It is how you would trust a component you did not write.** As models move into products, "it passed the evals" is the equivalent of "it passed the tests". Interpretability aims for the equivalent of code review.
- **It is young and open.** Many results come from small models and simple experiments. Much of the tooling, from visualisations to experiment runners, is ordinary frontend and backend engineering.

<Callout tone="caution" title="What it cannot do yet">
  Today's methods explain parts of models, not whole models. The researchers behind the results
  above are careful to describe their evidence as partial, and many of the clearest results come
  from small or toy models. Read claims about "understanding" a large model as "understanding one
  mechanism inside it".
</Callout>

## How this series works

Each of the next three posts takes one idea and gives you a model small enough to run in your browser:

1. **[Features and superposition](/posts/features-and-superposition):** train a model that squeezes 5 features into 2 numbers, and watch how it does it.
2. **[Sparse autoencoders](/posts/sparse-autoencoders):** recover those 5 features from the 2 numbers alone.
3. **[Induction heads](/posts/induction-heads):** break a working circuit, then repair it with a patch.

The models are toys: a few dozen parameters, trained or hand-written in plain TypeScript. They are small enough that you can check every number, which is exactly why researchers use toy models too.
